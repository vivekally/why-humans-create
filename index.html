A Multidisciplinary Inquiry

Why Do Humans Create?

Two questions: why the drive to build things runs so deep, and why what we build feels so much like ours. An answer drawn from evolutionary biology, psychology, archaeology and philosophy, with the seams left showing.

The Short Answers
Q1

Why do humans have such a deep drive to create?

Making things is older than our species. Stone tools appear at least 2.6 million years ago and possibly 3.3 million, and other primates flake stone too. What is distinctive about humans is less raw inventiveness than cumulative culture: we copy, teach and improve on what earlier generations built, so creation compounds.

At the individual level, creating satisfies the three needs Self-Determination Theory calls autonomy, competence and relatedness, and it reliably produces absorbed, high-concentration states (flow). Flow is not the same as feeling happy in the moment. People are in flow more often at work than at leisure and still say they would rather be elsewhere.

Why the drive exists at the deepest level is unsettled. Sexual selection (Miller), cognitive byproduct (Pinker), social bonding and credible signalling (the current debate in music evolution) all remain live.

Confidence: moderate on the archaeology and psychology, low on any single evolutionary explanation
Q2

Why does ownership over what we create feel so powerful?

The idea that labour makes something yours is old (Locke, 1689) and shows up early in development: preschoolers transfer ownership to whoever put creative work into an object, more strongly than adults do (Kanngiesser et al.). Adults overvalue what they assembled themselves even when it is worse (the IKEA effect), and the endowment effect appears in young children and in capuchin monkeys.

Pierce and colleagues' theory of psychological ownership names three routes to feeling something is "mine": controlling it, investing yourself in it, knowing it intimately. Creating does all three. Legacy motives (generativity, and in a more contested form Terror Management Theory) add an existential layer.

Whether this attachment is good for you is a separate question. The Gita's counsel to act without attachment to the fruits of action and the Buddhist warning against clinging are answers, not just alternatives.

Confidence: high that labour drives ownership feelings, low on the death-anxiety account

Section I

The Evolutionary Roots: Creation Before Homo sapiens

The archaeological record ties making things to deep time. The Lomekwi 3 stone tools in Kenya, dated by Harmand et al. (2015) to roughly 3.3 million years ago, predate the genus Homo. The finding is contested. Critics have questioned the stratigraphic context, and Proffitt et al. (2016) showed that wild capuchin monkeys produce sharp-edged flakes unintentionally while pounding stones, which blurs the line between "tool" and "debris." The less disputed Oldowan tools from Gona, Ethiopia (Semaw et al. 1997, about 2.6 million years) still put deliberate making far before anything resembling modern cognition.

That capuchin result matters for the question itself. Chimpanzees, crows and capuchins all make and use tools. So "why do humans create?" cannot be answered by pointing at creation. The useful question is what is different about human creation, and the strongest candidate answer is not individual inventiveness.

The Collective Brain

Joseph Henrich (The Secret of Our Success, 2015) and Michael Tomasello (The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition, 1999) argue that what makes human creation distinctive is cumulative cultural evolution: high-fidelity social learning that lets innovations ratchet upward across generations. Henrich goes further than "in addition to": he argues individual humans are not especially clever inventors, and that most technology emerges from many small, often unintended improvements accumulating across a population. On that view the drive to learn, imitate and teach does most of the work, and a strong individual "drive to create" is partly an illusion produced by looking at the finished artefact.

This is in tension with everything below that locates the drive inside the individual. It is not resolved here. Both can be true at different levels, but they are not simply additive.

More contested is why humans create things with no obvious survival utility: art, music, ornament. "Obvious" is doing work in that sentence, because the assumption that these are non-functional may itself be wrong.

Contested
Sexual Selection and The Mating Mind

Geoffrey Miller (2000) argued that creativity functions as a costly signal of genetic fitness, analogous to the peacock's tail, under Amotz Zahavi's (1975) handicap principle: signals are reliable because they are expensive.

Two problems. The handicap principle itself has been under sustained attack; Penn & Számadó (2020) argue it was elevated to a "principle" on flawed grounds and that cost is neither necessary nor sufficient for honest signalling. And Miller's theory predicts sex differences in creative display whose size and interpretation are disputed.

Steven Pinker (How the Mind Works, 1997) took the skeptical view, calling music "auditory cheesecake": a byproduct of systems evolved for language, auditory scene analysis, emotional calls, habitat selection and motor control, rather than an adaptation in its own right.

The Miller-versus-Pinker framing is now a quarter-century old and the field has moved. The current debate in music evolution is between a social bonding account (Savage et al. 2021) and a credible signalling account (Mehr et al. 2021), both published as target articles in the same journal issue. Neither is sexual selection and neither is cheesecake. Decisive evidence does not yet exist for any of the four.

Theory
Niche Construction

Odling-Smee, Laland & Feldman (2003): organisms modify environments, and those modifications change selection pressures on subsequent generations. Humans are the most dramatic niche constructors on earth, locked into a loop where creation begets dependence on creation.

Theory
Extended Phenotype, and Its Limit

Richard Dawkins (1982): genes express themselves not only in bodies but in what organisms build. A beaver's dam is as much a product of its genome as its teeth.

Dawkins restricted the idea to artefacts whose variation is under genetic influence, and explicitly routed human culture through a different replicator, the meme. Calling a startup or a symphony an "extended phenotype" is a metaphor he would reject. The concept explains why building is in our nature; it does not explain what we build.

Section II

The Psychology: Why We Create and Why We Cling

The Drive to Create

TheoryEmpirical
Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

Deci & Ryan (1985; Ryan & Deci 2000) identify three basic needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. Creative work is a natural setting for all three, though "creation satisfies all three" is an application of the theory, not a finding of it.

The part of this literature most relevant to startups and to ownership is the overjustification effect: external rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 meta-analysis; Amabile 1996 on creativity specifically). The size and generality of that effect were disputed in a long exchange with Cameron and Pierce in the 1990s, so it is best read as real but conditional.

TheoryEmpirical
Flow

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (1990): total absorption in a challenging-but-achievable task. Experience-sampling studies reliably find flow associated with high concentration, challenge and a sense of skill.

Flow is not reliably associated with feeling happy in the moment. Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre (1989) found people were in flow far more often at work than in leisure, yet reported wanting to be doing something else while at work. They called this the paradox of work. Any claim that creating is "one of the most reliable sources of happiness" overreads this literature.

The creation–wellbeing link is not uniformly positive. Kyaga et al. (2011) found that people with bipolar disorder, and healthy siblings of people with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder, were over-represented in creative occupations in Swedish registry data; a 2013 follow-up on 1.2 million people found the association held mainly for authors. These are occupational associations, not evidence that the drive is compulsive. The broader "mad genius" literature is heavily contested (Schlesinger 2009) and effect sizes are small.

The Attachment to What We've Created

Empirical
Creative Labour and Ownership in Children

Kanngiesser, Gjersoe & Hood (2010): when one person borrows another's modelling clay and sculpts it into something new, both preschoolers and adults tend to say the sculptor now owns it. Holding the object or making small changes does not have this effect. The effect was stronger in children than in adults. A follow-up in Japan and the UK (2014) found the labour rule in both cultures, with differences in how strongly it overrode prior ownership.

This is the most direct evidence on the second question: the feeling that creative work makes something yours appears early, before formal instruction in property, and across at least two quite different cultures.

Empirical
The IKEA Effect

Norton, Mochon & Ariely (2012): people value self-assembled objects more than identical objects assembled by others, and value their own amateur origami close to what experts' work fetches, while other people rate it near worthless. The authors link this to effectance motivation (Robert White 1959). Boundary conditions from the same paper: the effect disappears when participants fail to complete the object or when it is disassembled. The effect has replicated in adults and in children.

EmpiricalContested
The Endowment Effect

Owning a thing raises what you demand to give it up (Thaler 1980; Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1990). It appears in young children and in capuchin monkeys (Lakshminarayanan, Chen & Santos 2008), which suggests ownership feelings predate language and culture.

Plott & Zeiler (2005) argued the gap largely vanishes under procedures that remove subject misconceptions. Isoni, Loomes & Sugden (2011) replicated their procedures and found the gap persists for lotteries. Read it as real but more context-dependent than the textbook version.

Theory
Psychological Ownership

Pierce, Kostova & Dirks (2001, 2003) propose three routes to feeling something is "mine": controlling it, investing yourself in it, and coming to know it intimately. These are theory papers; the measurement work came later. That creation engages all three routes at once is a plausible application of the model, and the Kanngiesser and IKEA results are consistent with it, but "attachment to made things surpasses attachment to bought things" has not been tested as such.

The Existential Dimension

Theory
Generativity and Legacy

Erikson (1950) named generativity, the midlife concern with creating and guiding what will outlast you, as a developmental task. McAdams & de St. Aubin (1992) built a measure and found it predicts creative and civic behaviour. This is the less dramatic, better-supported account of why we want our creations to persist.

Contested
Terror Management Theory (TMT)

Greenberg, Pyszczynski & Solomon (1986), building on Ernest Becker's The Denial of Death (1973): awareness of mortality creates anxiety managed through cultural worldviews and self-esteem, and creation offers symbolic immortality.

The core experimental paradigm, mortality salience producing worldview defence, failed to replicate in Many Labs 4 (Klein et al. 2022, preprint 2019), with and without original-author involvement. The creation-as-immortality claim was not directly tested there, but it rests on the same paradigm, so it inherits the problem rather than escaping it.

A Known Debate, Not a New One

SDT frames creation as approach-motivated (toward growth). TMT frames it as avoidance-motivated (away from death anxiety). These were argued directly against each other in the same journal issue: Pyszczynski et al. (2004) claimed TMT explains why SDT's needs exist, and Ryan & Deci (2004) replied that meaning-seeking cannot be reduced to defence, and that esteem built on defence is contingent esteem, not the real thing. Twenty years on, the replication record has weakened the TMT side without settling the question.

Section III

The Anthropological and Historical Record

The Blombos Cave engraved ochre in South Africa (about 75,000 years, Henshilwood et al. 2002; a crosshatched drawing at 73,000 years, 2018) and the Chauvet paintings in France (about 36,000 years, Quiles et al. 2016) are the textbook cases of early symbolic making. The record has moved past them in ways that bear on the question:

  • Figurative cave art in Sulawesi, Indonesia, is now dated to at least 51,000 years (Oktaviana et al. 2024), older than anything in Europe.
  • Red pigment marks in three Spanish caves have been dated to roughly 64,000 years, before Homo sapiens reached Iberia, and attributed to Neanderthals (Hoffmann et al. 2018). The dating is disputed (White et al. 2020). If it holds, the drive to mark and decorate is not a sapiens story.
  • A zigzag engraved on a freshwater shell at Trinil, Java, is around half a million years old and attributed to Homo erectus (Joordens et al. 2014).

We should be cautious about reading any of these through modern categories. We do not know whether they served communicative, ritual or mnemonic functions that were directly useful.

Donald Brown's list of human universals (1991) includes toolmaking, art, music, body adornment, narrative and play. It also includes property and inheritance rules, which bears on the second question. The list is a compilation from the ethnographic record rather than a systematic survey, so "in every documented culture" should be read as "no documented exception," which is weaker.

Attachment Is Not Only Questioned Outside the West

The psychological literature above mostly assumes that attachment to one's creations is natural and, implicitly, healthy. Several traditions treat that as the problem. The Bhagavad Gita (2.47) counsels nishkama karma: you have a right to your work but never to its fruits, and acting for the fruits is the trap. Buddhist thought names upadana, clinging, as a link in the chain that produces suffering. The Daoist wu wei (Slingerland 2014 is a good modern treatment) prizes effortless action over striving. Stoic and Christian ascetic traditions say similar things in different vocabulary, so this is not a West-versus-East divide. It is a divide between a descriptive psychology of attachment and prescriptive traditions that say the attachment should be loosened.

These traditions do not deny the attachment exists. They are evidence that people in very different cultures noticed it, found it strong, and built practices to manage it.

The legal notion of individual intellectual property is usually dated to the Statute of Anne (1710), though Venice granted patents from 1474 and the Roman poet Martial was complaining about plagiarism in the first century. Legal ownership of creations is recent; the felt claim is not. Karl Marx's theory of alienation (1844) argued that separating workers from the products of their labour is damaging. That narrow claim is consistent with psychological ownership research; Marx's broader claims about class and species-being have no experimental parallel.

Lewis Mumford, in Technics and Civilization (1934), traced how technical phases reshape the relationship between makers and their work, including the shift from craft to mechanised production. Reading modern startup culture as an attempt to reunify identity with creation is a natural extension of that argument, but it is ours, not his.

Section IV

Creating vs. Owning: Pulling Them Apart

The drive to create appears rooted in intrinsic motivation, effectance and flow: process, agency, competence. The claim to own what you create has its own lineage. John Locke (1689) argued that mixing your labour with something makes it yours; that is the philosophical root of the intuition, and the Kanngiesser results suggest four-year-olds already hold it. On top sit the endowment effect, psychological ownership, identity extension (William James's "material self," 1890), and legacy.

One distinction does most of the work in what follows: legal ownership (who may sell, license or exclude) and psychological ownership (who feels it is theirs) are different things that usually travel together and sometimes come apart.

Creation without legal ownership. Open-source developers, ghostwriters and oral storytellers create without owning in the legal sense. They rarely lack psychological ownership. Maintainers speak of "my project," ghostwriters report resentment at unattributed work, and open-source burnout is often described in the language of a possession that has become a burden. The cases show creation persisting without legal title, not without the feeling.

Ownership without creation. Inherited wealth, purchased art, acquired companies. Pierce et al.'s model predicts this ownership feels thinner because two of the three routes (investment of self, intimate knowledge) are missing. Plausible, and consistent with the labour findings, but untested in those terms.

Founders. The question named startups, and there is a literature. Cardon et al. (2005) documented how pervasively founders describe ventures as children ("my baby"), and (2009) formalised entrepreneurial passion as identity-centred: the venture is not just owned but is part of who the founder is. This is the strongest everyday case of creation, legal ownership and identity fusing, and it explains why losing control of a company can feel like a bereavement rather than a transaction.

Where they conflict. IP disputes and work-for-hire are the clean cases: the law assigns ownership to one party while the psychology stays with the maker. The cost is real and the two systems can be separated on purpose, which is what employment contracts do.

Creation and destruction are not opposites. Schumpeter's "creative destruction" (1942) is an economic claim about how innovation displaces incumbents, not a psychological one; Bakunin's "the passion for destruction is a creative passion" (1842) is a slogan, not a finding. The observation that making often requires unmaking is fair; there is no body of evidence behind it that we have found.
Synthesis

Tying the Threads Together

Making things is older than our species and shared with other animals. What is distinctive about humans is that making compounds: we learn from each other well enough that each generation starts from the last one's ceiling. Whether the individual urge to build is itself a selected trait, a signal, a byproduct or mostly an artefact of that cultural ratchet is not settled, and the strongest recent work (Henrich) leans toward the last of these.

The feeling that what you made is yours is on firmer ground. It appears in preschoolers, in two cultures at least, in adults who overvalue their own furniture, and in the founders who call companies their children. Locke wrote it down; the psychology mostly confirms he was describing something real. Whether that feeling should be indulged is a different question, and several old traditions answer no.

What Remains Uncertain

Why the drive exists at the deepest evolutionary level. How much of it is individual and how much collective. Whether the death-anxiety account survives its replication record. How far the labour-makes-ownership rule generalises beyond the cultures where it has been tested. This page presents the tensions between approach and avoidance, individual and collective, attachment and non-attachment as open, because they are.
